FAQ – Registration

When is the Maintainer Summit taking place?

Sunday, November 8, one day prior to KubeCon + CloudNativeCon North America 2026.

Where is the Maintainer Summit located?

Salt Palace Convention Center | 90 South West Temple, Salt Lake City, UT 84101
Badge Pick-Up is located at the South Entrance.

Who can attend the Maintainer Summit?

  • Sandbox Project Maintainers (as listed in project-maintainers.csv)
  • Incubating & Graduated Project GitHub Org Members
  • CNCF TAG Leads & the CNCF TOC

Why do I need to be a CNCF Project GitHub Org member to attend?

We welcome everyone to start contributing to a project, but with 7,500+ CNCF Project Org members and limited space, we’ve decided to limit attendance to those who are already actively engaged a project.

I’m not listed as someone who could attend, but I contribute, what should I do?

We encourage you to continue to contribute and pursue your project’s path for becoming a GitHub Org member or climbing the contributor ladder to become a listed maintainer. Until then, there are plenty of other opportunities to get involved at KubeCon + CloudNativeCon. Check out the schedule and get involved.

What is a sponsored attendee?

  • If you are a Maintainer, TAG Chair, Tech Lead, K8s SIG Lead, or WG Lead, and there is someone who is not yet a GH org member, you can sponsor them to attend the summit. If you are sponsoring an attendee, proactively contact [email protected] with the individual’s name, company, and community participation details.
  • The sponsored attendee must indicate that they are a sponsored attendee during registration and share the name of the individual sponsoring them.
  • Sponsored attendees will be reviewed weekly, note not all sponsored attendees will be granted registration.

How will I know if I’m registered for the Maintainer Summit?

After registering, your registration will be placed into a pending queue for review. Once a week, CNCF staff will validate that you meet the criteria to attend and will either approve or deny your registration. If you believe a mistake was made, please contact [email protected].

If I’m accepted as a Maintainer Summit speaker – do I also have to purchase registration for KubeCon + CloudNativeCon?

No, all CFP-submitted accepted maintainer summit speakers will be provided with an “All-Access” pass for CNCF-hosted co-located events and KubeCon + CloudNativeCon programming. (Note: This does not include proposals for Project Meetings)

I’m leading an unconference session at the summit. Do I need to purchase registration for KubeCon and CloudNativeCon?

Yes, due to the short time frame for planning unconference sessions, complimentary registration will not be provided.

Can I attend virtually?

Unfortunately, no, there is not an opportunity to attend virtually.

Do I need to register for KubeCon + CloudNativeCon to attend the summit?

Yes, to attend The Maintainer Summit, you must be registered for KubeCon + CloudNativeCon.

Is there maintainer support for attending?

Please apply for the Dan Kohn Maintainer Scholarship. Scholarship information is coming soon!

Will there be a registration option only to attend the Maintainer Summit and not KubeCon + CloudNativeCon?

No, there is NOT a single-day Maintainer Summit-only pass available.

Keynote: How the CNCF Project Maturity Journey Makes Projects Stronger
Salt Palace | Level 2 | 250 A-C Jared Watts, Cortney Nickerson
Keynote Sessions ↓ Slides
9:15 AM – 9:30 AM

When Crossplane graduated within the CNCF late last year, and Kyverno followed shortly after, the votes marked the culmination of years of work to mature each project and meet the expectations of the TOC. While both projects ultimately reached the same milestone, their journeys looked quite different. In this session, maintainers from Crossplane and Kyverno walk through what it really took to get there.

Crossplane’s first graduation application wasn't quite ready: the TOC raised concerns about vendor neutrality, long-term survivability, and the health of our extension ecosystem. We'll share how the project put this feedback into action by launching a vendor-neutral community registry, migrating release infrastructure to CNCF-owned accounts, and instituting new governance and lifecycle policies for our ecosystem.

Kyverno's first graduation push wasn’t ready either, however their path presented a different set of challenges. We'll discuss how the project addressed questions around maintainer diversity and long-term project sustainability, strengthened the contributor community across organizations, worked through technical, governance, and security due diligence, and partnered closely with the TOC throughout the process.

Together, these experiences highlight that graduation is not about following a single checklist. It's about demonstrating that a project has the technical maturity, governance, and community needed to thrive for years to come.

We'll also cover the follow-up commitments both projects made to the TOC after graduation, because the work of maturing a project never really ends.

Wherever your project sits on the maturity curve, come hear what we went through, the lessons we learned, and how you can make the CNCF maturity process work for your project and community.

Reviewing AI-Generated PRs When Every Bug Is a CVE: Envoy's Playbook
Salt Palace | Level 2 | 250 A-C Rohit Agrawal, Kateryna Nezdolii
Main Track ↓ Slides
10:15 AM – 10:50 AM

Envoy sits in the request path of much of the internet. A bug that slips through review can become a CVE with a coordinated disclosure, an embargo, and patch releases across supported versions. That math changed when AI-generated pull requests arrived. They look polished, they compile, they pass CI, and they are wrong in ways human contributions rarely are.

This talk shares the review playbook Envoy maintainers built in response. We cover the failure patterns we see in AI-written changes, how we adjusted reviewer expectations without banning AI outright, the signals that separate a careful contributor using AI from drive-by slop, and how our security team treats these changes in sensitive code paths like parsers and filters.

Attendees leave with a review checklist, contributor policy language they can copy, and real numbers on what this costs maintainer time. Every lesson applies to any project where a bad merge has consequences beyond a revert.

Health Check Hell: A Call to Standardize CRD Health Across the Ecosystem
Salt Palace | Level 2 | 250 D-F Regina Voloshin, Dag Bjerre Andersen
Main Track ↓ Slides
10:15 AM – 10:50 AM

After 10 years of Kubernetes, maintainers across the ecosystem still independently answer the same question: is this resource healthy? Every CRD defines status, conditions, and readiness differently, so every tool – Argo CD, Flux, kubectl plugins, dashboards, CI/CD systems – ends up reimplementing health interpretation for the same resources, causing duplicated work, inconsistent behavior, and operational drift.

This session is a working discussion. Together, we'll explore what a shared specification for CRD health and readiness could look like, building on efforts such as kstatus. We'll discuss how conformance should be achieved: through a shared library, independent implementations against a common specification and conformance suite, or another approach. The goal is to reach consensus on whether this is worth pursuing formally, for example through a CNCF Sandbox project or working group, and who's willing to help drive it.

Source Control Is Not Enough: Owning the Distribution Path
Salt Palace | Level 2 | 250 D-F Vadim Bauer
CI / CD / Distribution
11:00 AM – 11:35 AM

CNCF has a well-established model for source-code stewardship. But once a release is cut, many projects hand critical parts of their relationship with users to infrastructure they do not control.

Images, charts, and other artifacts are published through a patchwork of vendor-operated registries. Once registry addresses appear in manifests, policies, mirrors, and downstream products, changing them becomes an ecosystem-wide operation.

This talk examines distribution as an overlooked part of open source governance. The distribution channel defines an artifact’s canonical identity, availability, retention, and trust metadata. It also determines whether a project can change providers without disrupting users.

Drawing on our experience as Harbor maintainers, we will give maintainers a framework for deciding which parts of the distribution path must remain under project or foundation control, which can be delegated, and where CNCF may need a shared strategy.

Unconference: Evolving Kubernetes for Agentic Workloads: Rethinking the Pod and Node Boundary
Salt Palace | Level 2 | 250 A-C
Unconference Sessions
2:20 PM – 2:55 PM

Target Audience & Relevant Projects

Kubernetes Maintainers & Contributors: SIG Node, SIG Scheduling, SIG Architecture, SIG Storage, SIG Network
Container Runtimes & Sandboxing: containerd, CRI-O, Kata Containers, gVisor, Cloud Hypervisor
AI & Agent Platforms: Engineers building high-density, dynamic agent executors or sandbox environments

Problem Statement

Autonomous AI agents represent the fastest-growing workload category in cloud infrastructure, yet their operational profile inverts almost every fundamental assumption behind Kubernetes container orchestration:

Extreme Idleness: Agent sessions spend 90–95%+ of their lifespan idle waiting on model inference, tool I/O, or human approval, punctuated by high burstiness on wake-up.
Extreme Density: Production systems require packing 500–1000+ isolated micro-sandboxes per host rather than the traditional ~110 Pods per node.

Sub-Second Latencies: End users and RL agent loops demand sub-second sandbox resumption (P95 ≤ 500ms), far faster than traditional Pod scheduling cycles.

Untrusted Code Execution: Agents execute arbitrary, model-generated code next to sensitive tools and credentials, demanding lightweight hardware-level or kernel isolation (microVMs/kata/gVisor).

Because the Kubernetes Pod contract is fixed at creation time and every state change writes through etcd, emerging agentic frameworks (such as Agent Substrate, and many others) are increasingly routing around Kubernetes APIs on their hot paths, building custom out-of-tree control planes, running nested runtimes, and treating Kubernetes merely as an over-provisioned VM manager.

If Kubernetes does not evolve its process layer, the cloud-native ecosystem risks fracturing into proprietary sandbox fabrics.

Proposed Discussion Topic

This session will bring together maintainers across Kubernetes and the agentic ecosystem to align on how the core primitives should evolve:

Mapping the Frictions:
Where does the current Pod contract break down for agentic workloads?
Walkthrough of real-world workarounds: bypassing etcd, nested sandboxing, warm worker pools, and memory stranding.
Kubernetes as the Distributed Kernel:
Defining the boundary: What belongs in Kubernetes (Process/Kernel layer) vs. what belongs in agent frameworks (Userspace)?
The "Two-Level Control" model: How can the Kubelet delegate local actor/container management within a dynamic Pod envelope without overwhelming etcd?
Concrete Upstream Touchpoints:
SIG Node & CRI: Unprivileged sandboxing/microVMs (VM-as-a-container), dynamic container creation/destruction, and native suspend/resume/freeze lifecycle states.
Resource Management: Pod-level resource sharing, in-place resizing, workload-controlled swap/MGLRU, and node overcommit for idle states.
Networking & Identity: Dynamic endpoints and wake-on-traffic routing for suspended actors.
Action Items & Working Group Alignment:
Can we establish a shared cross-SIG/cross-project effort (e.g., under SIG Node / TAG Runtime) to formalize agentic requirements into KEPs?
Desired Outcomes
A shared understanding of agentic workload requirements across Kubernetes and sandbox runtime maintainers.
Agreement on architectural boundaries (what K8s should standardize vs. what runtimes/frameworks should handle).
Identification of concrete KEP targets and collaboration channels for 2027 development cycles.

Session leads: Dawn Chen and Antonio Ojea

Unconference Session: GPU Sandboxes in gVisor: What's Missing and What's Next

Unconference Session: Trust & Triage in the AI Era
Salt Palace | Level 2 | 251 A-C
Unconference Sessions
2:20 PM – 2:55 PM

Sustaining trusted open source projects in this new environment raises several pressing questions, with two core challenges standing out:

Trust: Maintaining and deepening trust with both users and contributors.
Triage: Cutting through increased noise to identify valuable contributions and high-priority bugs as incoming volume accelerates.
I don't think 1) is solvable without some component of 2).

How do we build genuine trust when pull requests and review comments are increasingly automated? How do we safeguard against future supply-chain threats like the xz utils backdoor in this environment?

What does advancement on the contributor ladder mean when a maintainer spends hours reviewing code, only for the author to prompt an AI agent to address the feedback? Who actually performed the work, and who gained a deep understanding of the project's codebase and architecture?

How do we effectively filter incoming changes when individual developers can generate massive volumes of pull requests with minimal effort? What stops malicious actors from trivially gaming these workflows, and how do we ensure legitimate contributors remain visible through the noise?

While large-scale projects like Kubernetes have faced similar challenges before, the scale and velocity are escalating rapidly.

In my opinion these questions are far more critical than surface-level metrics like PR merge velocity or initial review response times. Throughput does not equate to value or quality.

We must maintain confidence among our community and downstream users that we are building a secure, high-quality infrastructure foundation.

We need strategies to cut through low-effort automated submissions, highlight high-value contributions, and adapt how we mentor and cultivate future maintainers.

The goal of this session is to brainstorm actionable solutions and guidance for CNCF project communities.

Session Leads: Benjamin Elder & Maciej Szulik

Unconference: Graceful Node Shutdown: what it should mean, and how workload controllers find out
Salt Palace | Level 2 | 250 A-C
Unconference Sessions
3:05 PM – 3:40 PM

What projects are involved?
Graceful Node Shutdown: what it should mean, and how workload controllers find out

What projects are involved?

Kubernetes: SIG Node, SIG Apps, SIG Cloud Provider, SIG Windows, WG Node Lifecycle

Target Audience & Relevant Projects
Kubernetes maintainers and contributors: SIG Node, SIG Apps, SIG Cloud Provider, SIG Windows, WG Node Lifecycle
Operators who run long shutdown windows (30 minutes or more) with per-priority termination, or who have seen DaemonSets not come back after a reboot
Anyone building drain or maintenance tooling on top of Node Lifecycle Conditions (Specialized Lifecycle Management kubernetes/enhancements#5683)
Problem Statement
Graceful Node Shutdown (kubernetes/enhancements#2000) has been beta and on by default since v1.21; priority-based shutdown (kubernetes/enhancements#2712) since v1.24. Neither has moved since. Writing the follow-up KEPs this year showed that the semantics were never settled and that nothing outside the node can see a shutdown happening:

The kubelet terminates by priority but admits by nothing. shutdownGracePeriodByPodPriority ends Pods in tiers, lowest priority first. Admit() rejects every new Pod for the whole window, on Linux and Windows. A system-critical Pod that needs to be created during the user-workload tier is rejected like everything else (Graceful Node Shutdown should allow admission of high priority pods kubernetes/kubernetes#142521).
The DaemonSet controller does not know. It creates a Pod on the node, the kubelet rejects it, the controller deletes it and creates another, until the node is gone (DaemonSet controller and Graceful Node Shutdown manager disagree when making workloads placement decision kubernetes/kubernetes#122912, EKS 1.34 DaemonSet Controller created a lot of pod during node deletion kubernetes/kubernetes#137895).
Nothing leaves the node. Node Lifecycle Conditions landed in v1.37 as admin-managed conditions. No kubelet writes GracefulNodeShutdownInProgress yet, so controllers, autoscalers and drain tooling have no signal to read.
The failure modes are not written down. A kubelet restarted mid-shutdown has no record that one was in progress, and operators have reported DaemonSets that did not restart after a GNS reboot.
Short window or long one? Is GNS a short window where nothing new runs, or a 30-plus-minute priority-ordered termination? The two answers want different APIs, and the same question applies to drain.
Proposed Discussion Topic
The plan today: tier-aware admission in the kubelet (v1.39, under the KEP-2000 update, with KEP-2712 updated alongside); the kubelet setting GracefulNodeShutdownInProgress and publishing the priority boundary it is terminating at; a DaemonSet controller reader that holds back Pods below that boundary (SLM: Publish Graceful Node Shutdown state for DaemonSet coordination kubernetes/enhancements#6249); GNS as an EvictionRequest requester (EvictionRequest API kubernetes/enhancements#4563) as the graduation step. The boundary needs a carrier, and there are two paths:
A) PodDeletionPreference. The kubelet writes a priorityBelow rule that any controller can read, so the published API is not GNS-specific and the same object serves scale-down scoring later. Depends on the PDP KEP landing with a priority-threshold rule and node scoping.
B) A GNS-specific Node status signal. The kubelet publishes the boundary on the Node object (for example a kubelet-owned HighestPriorityAllowed field). No new dependency, but SIG Node carries a GNS-only Node API and every consumer learns a GNS-specific field.
Which parts of that plan belong to the kubelet, which to workload controllers, and which to drain tooling
Who else should consume the signal: StatefulSet rollouts (SLM: StatefulSet rollouts respect Node lifecycle conditions kubernetes/enhancements#6265), Job placement (SLM: Job Pod replacement during node maintenance kubernetes/enhancements#6262), scale-down scoring
Several writers of the same Node condition (kubelet, kubectl drain, cluster autoscaler, admins): who sets, who clears, what happens when the node never returns (SLM: Coordinate Node lifecycle condition writers kubernetes/enhancements#6430)
What GA for KEP-2000 and KEP-2712 should require
Desired Outcomes
A written answer to the short-window versus priority-ordered question, or agreement on which SIG owns the answer
Agreement on the carrier for shutdown state, so the KEP-2000 update and KEP-6249 can go to review for v1.39 without reopening it
A list of consumers of the signal with a name next to each
A first cut of GA criteria for KEP-2000 and KEP-2712

Session Leads: Daniel Bruno, Ryan Hallisey, and Filip Křepinský

Unconference: More PRs, same maintainers. What's helping in the AI era, and what's not?
Salt Palace | Level 2 | 251 A-C
Unconference Sessions
3:05 PM – 3:40 PM

What projects are involved?
Any CNCF project, including Kubernetes and its subprojects, e.g. agent-sandbox, Kueue, JobSet

Description
This is a follow-up to the Maintainer Summit EU 2026 session "How should maintainers navigate (and review) AI-based PRs?". Since March, projects have tried several approaches/tools:

stricter disclosure rules and closing low-effort PRs faster
AI pre-review bots (default-on or human-summoned) and its policy
growing reviewers and delegating ownership across code, docs, and releases
writing down conventions that used to live in maintainers' heads (CONTRIBUTING.md, AGENTS.md)
linters, validation checks, and release or CI flake automation
checked-in agent skills (either centrally in maintainer-tools repo or in each subproject repo directory)
Maintainers from Kubernetes subprojects (agent-sandbox, Kueue, JobSet) will share what we tried and how we measured it. We want to hear from other projects too, including release, docs, and security folks dealing with what happens after PRs merge.

We don't pitch or endorse any tool. We want to compare what happened in practice, where things broke, and how much depended on repo size or contributor mix. If your project tried any of this (whether you kept it or turned it off), tell us what you tried, how you could tell whether it helped, and the gotchas.

We'll post a summary back here afterward:

approaches/tools that helped maintainers, with gotchas, and when they apply (e.g. repo size, contributor mix)
how to tell if it's working, e.g. time to first maintainer response (contributor wait time), maintainer hours per release, checks that quality didn't drop (reverts, human review depth)
Out of scope: generic debates about whether AI is good or bad, or org-wide policy mandates.

Session leads: Janet Kuo and Kevin Hannon
